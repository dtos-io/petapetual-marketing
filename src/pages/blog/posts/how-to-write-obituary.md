An obituary is often one of the first things written after someone passes. In the midst of grief and funeral arrangements, you're asked to summarize a lifetime in a few paragraphs. It's a daunting task.

But an obituary is also a gift. It's a public declaration of a life that mattered. Done well, it captures the essence of who someone was, not just the facts of their existence.

This guide will help you write an obituary that truly honors your loved one.

## What is an Obituary?

An obituary serves multiple purposes:
- **Announcement:** Informs the community of the death
- **Biography:** Summarizes key facts of their life
- **Tribute:** Celebrates who they were
- **Information:** Shares funeral/memorial service details

Some obituaries are primarily factual. Others are more personal. Many blend both approaches.

## The Basic Structure

While there's no single correct format, most obituaries include:

### 1. The Opening

Announce the death with the person's full name, age, location, and date of death. You may mention the cause of death or simply state they passed "peacefully" or "after a long illness."

Example:
> "John Michael Smith, 78, of Portland, Oregon, passed away peacefully on December 10, 2025, surrounded by family."

### 2. Life Overview

Provide a biographical sketch:
- Date and place of birth
- Parents' names
- Education
- Career highlights
- Military service
- Marriage and family

### 3. Personality and Passions

This is where the obituary comes alive. Go beyond facts:
- What were they passionate about?
- What did they love to do?
- What was their personality like?
- What will people remember about them?

### 4. Survivors

List surviving family members:
- Spouse
- Children (and spouses)
- Grandchildren
- Siblings
- Other significant relationships

### 5. Preceded in Death

Mention close family members who died before them.

### 6. Service Information

Provide details about:
- Funeral or memorial service (date, time, location)
- Visitation hours
- Burial or interment details
- Reception information

### 7. Donations or Memorials

If the family prefers donations to flowers, list preferred charities or causes.

## Tips for Writing a Meaningful Obituary

### Capture Their Essence

Don't just list facts—convey who they were:
- Instead of "She enjoyed gardening," try "Her rose garden was legendary, and she was known to rescue struggling plants that neighbors had given up on."

### Use Specific Details

Specific details are memorable:
- "He never missed a Mariners game"
- "She made the world's best apple pie—and everyone who tasted it agreed"
- "He had a joke for every occasion, especially the inappropriate ones"

### Include Their Voice

If possible, include something in their own words—a favorite saying, a piece of advice they often gave, their life philosophy.

### Balance Grief and Celebration

Obituaries can acknowledge the pain of loss while also celebrating the life lived. Both sadness and joy have a place.

### Get Input

Ask family members for stories and details. You might discover aspects of their life you didn't know.

### Read It Aloud

Hearing the words helps you catch awkward phrasing and ensures it flows naturally.

## Example Passages

**Capturing personality:**
> "Robert never met a stranger. Within five minutes of meeting anyone, he'd have them laughing and feeling like an old friend. He collected people the way others collect stamps—each one a treasure."

**Showing impact:**
> "As a teacher for 35 years at Lincoln High, Margaret shaped thousands of lives. Former students still reach out to thank her for believing in them when no one else did."

**Including humor (if appropriate):**
> "Tom would want you to know that he fought cancer with the same stubbornness he applied to everything—right up until the disease discovered it had underestimated him one too many times."

## Length Considerations

Newspaper obituaries often have word limits and associated costs. Online memorials have no such constraints. Consider:

- **Short newspaper version:** Key facts and service information
- **Extended online version:** Full life story with personality and details

## Beyond the Obituary

An obituary is just the beginning. It's a snapshot written in a difficult moment.

An online memorial allows for a fuller portrait over time. You can add:
- More stories as you remember them
- Photos that capture their life
- Contributions from others who knew them
- A living tribute that grows
